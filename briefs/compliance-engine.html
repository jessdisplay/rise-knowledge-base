Brief № 1

You own the compliance engine. This brief is self-contained: where the product is, the plan it sits inside, what already exists, what the dossier specifies, and exactly what to build. Every number is measured from the repositories at dev or counted in the document suite on this site.

01Context

Where the product actually is

Shared context, so this brief stands on its own.

Rise OS is real and running on staging, not a prototype. Sixteen route groups, ten front end feature modules, thirteen back end modules, 62 migrations. Authentication with mandatory 2FA and passkeys, organisations and tenancy, the legislation model with the NDIS Practice Standards seeded, the obligations engine, the expected documents catalogue, a Tiptap document editor with version history and real time collaboration, comments anchored into document content, and a full auditor workspace with cross-tenant grants, submission packs and findings. All of that is built.

The database enforces tenant isolation at row level through a three-role model (rise_app, rise_admin, rise_auth). Staging runs on Cloud Run behind a Google IAP gate with Cloud SQL over private IP.

Production does not exist yet. The infrastructure is written and turnkey but blocked on three GCP account actions only Jesse can perform. That is his item, not anyone else's.

One open question sits above this brief: whether Rise serves providers, auditors, or both. It is genuinely unresolved. Nothing in this brief depends on how it resolves, because every item here makes the compliance chain more complete and more provable, which is required either way. Do not wait on it and do not try to settle it.

03Yours

What you own, and why it is you

You own the engine that turns legislation into a provider's actual obligations, and turns their documents into defensible proof against those obligations. Modelling and AI systems are your strength and prompt work is not where that belongs.

You are not starting from scratch. A working engine exists and it is good. Your job is depth: closing the distance between what the platform models today and what the dossier already specifies.

Read the watch list before you model anything

The dossier keeps an explicit register of its own unresolved problems. Several are exactly the kind of thing that silently corrupts a data model if you assume them away:

  • Four standards have no implementing document, M2-2, M2-4, M2-6 and M2A-3. Your schema has to tolerate a standard with nothing under it, because four of them are real. This is also listed as an open item in the knowledge base's own CLAUDE.md.
  • Six policies have no downstream chain, POL-COC-01, POL-GOV-02, POL-GOV-03, POL-RGT-01, POL-RGT-02 and POL-WHS-01. The register owner confirms intent. Do not invent the edges.
  • The 2026 amendment layer is only partly sighted, IS26, PRPS26, QI26 and AQAR25. Only AQAR25's register ID is confirmed, F2025L01383.
  • SIL clauses are TBC and need re-verifying against F2026C00528.
  • 70 versus 66. Know which number you are using and why.
The standing rule, in the collection's own words

Never reconstruct clause numbers, section numbers, register IDs, F-numbers, counts or quotes from memory. Retrieve from a file in the repo or a fetched primary source, or leave an explicit [GAP]. Label claims verified, corroborated or design opinion. It is not legal advice, and where anything disagrees with the instrument on the Federal Register, the instrument governs.

The number that makes the register gap concrete

The suite on this site contains 19 registers. The platform schema can hold zero of them. A register is how a provider proves a control actually operates rather than merely existing, and it is the first thing an auditor asks for by name.

The most interesting problem in the product

ADR 0011 chose flat node linking with inferred document relationships. The dossier declares nine typed relationships and grades every claim.

Inference produces a link. It does not produce a defensible reason for the link, and the defensible reason is the product. An auditor does not want to know a policy is associated with a clause; they want to know it exists to meet it, and they want the citation.

Whether 0011 should be superseded is your call to make and to write up. If it should stand, say why and we keep it. A well-argued no is a good outcome.

07The work

Three pieces, in this order

FirstADR 0017, before any code

Map the platform's chain against the dossier's, take a position on each problem below, and say which we build first and why. Written before building so we argue with the reasoning rather than with a migration.

  • Where the two models agree, disagree, and where the dossier is ahead of us.
  • Registers: schema shape, and how they relate to expected documents.
  • Improvement actions: what closes one, and who can close it.
  • Typed relationships versus ADR 0011's inference. Supersede or uphold, with reasons.
  • Where the seed datasets should live, given they are currently on one laptop.
Done when the ADR is merged and someone who was not in the room can follow the argument.
SecondRegisters

The 19 register types listed in section 05, modelled so an auditor can be shown that a control operated on a date, by a person, against a clause. Start from the TPL-REG template rather than a blank schema. Expect expiry and cadence to matter: the schema already carries expiry_cadence and expiry_on, so look at how those are used first.

Done when a Harmony Care register can be filled in, linked to the clause it serves, and appear in a submission pack.
ThirdImprovement actions

A finding carries forward into a corrective action with an owner, a due date and a closure state, and the loop is traceable in both directions. REG-QMS-01 Continuous Improvement is the specification.

Done when a finding can be traced forward to a closed improvement action and backward to the clause that caused it.
08House rules

Constraints you have to work inside

These are already the team's standards, written down in the repo. They are not mine and they are good.

  • No ORM. Kysely over pg, builder API first. A sql fragment is a last resort, must be parameterised, as small as possible, and must carry a one-line comment saying why the builder cannot express it. An unjustified escaped-SQL fragment is a blocking review finding. This applies to any agent you spawn as well.
  • Self-contained migrations (ADR 0008). Each writes its own PK, RLS, policies, grants and tenant contract inline. No shared helpers. A later edit must never change what an applied migration produced.
  • Row-level security, three roles. Anything you add is tenant-scoped by default. Auditor cross-tenant access has its own ADR, 0014. Read it before touching anything an auditor can see.
  • Tests seed and assert through Kysely over the role pools, not raw pool.query(). test/document-version-state-schema.e2e-spec.ts shows the patterns.
  • Branch <type>/<kebab-desc> off dev, PR into dev, Conventional Commits.
  • The BE owns the API contract. After any DTO change the FE resyncs with pnpm gen:api; never hand-edit app/lib/api/gen/.
09Judgement

How this gets judged

  • The ADR lands first. Code before the argument is the failure mode here.
  • Coverage matches the engine's own standard. 758 lines of spec against 494 lines of service is the bar you are working next to.
  • Provenance survives. A claim must be able to say where it came from, the way the entity rules still report class 118 after rewriting the targets.
  • A good no counts. If ADR 0011 should stand, the deliverable is the argument.

Ask, do not guess

If the dossier and the codebase disagree, that is a real finding and it goes to Jesse rather than getting resolved silently in a migration. The dossier is a specification written by people, and parts of it are ahead of what has been agreed commercially.
