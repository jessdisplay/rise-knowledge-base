Brief № 1

You own the compliance engine. This is the hardest modelling problem in the product and the part a competitor cannot copy by looking at our screens. Everything below is measured from the repository at dev or taken from the Rise dossier, with the source named so you can check any number yourself.

01Assignment

What you own, and why it is you

You own the engine that turns legislation into a provider's actual obligations, and turns their documents into defensible proof against those obligations. Modelling and AI systems are your strength and prompt work is not where that belongs.

You are not starting from scratch. A working engine exists and it is good. Your job is depth: closing the distance between what the platform models today and what the Rise dossier already specifies.

One thing to be explicit about, because it will save you time. There is an open strategic question about whether Rise serves providers, auditors, or both, and it is unresolved above this brief. Nothing in this brief depends on how it resolves. Every item here makes the compliance chain more complete and more provable, which is required either way. Do not wait on it and do not try to settle it.

Against instrument NDIS_PRPS_RULES_2018, addressed by ordinal NodeRef (instrumentCode, moduleOrd, partOrd, clauseOrd; containers carry trailing zero ordinals):

That is real statutory nuance, correctly modelled, with tests. It is the standard to match.

Read the watch list before you model anything

The dossier keeps an explicit list of its own moving parts. These are unresolved problems, not background reading, and several of them are exactly the kind of thing that silently corrupts a data model if you assume them away:

  • Four standards have no implementing document, M2-2, M2-4, M2-6, M2A-3. Visible on the map as teal boxes with no coral line. Your schema has to tolerate a standard with nothing under it, because four of them are real.
  • Six policies have no downstream chain, POL-COC-01, POL-GOV-02, POL-GOV-03, POL-RGT-01, POL-RGT-02, POL-WHS-01. The register owner has to confirm intent or the edges get wired. Do not invent the edges.
  • The 2026 amendment layer is only partly sighted, IS26, PRPS26, QI26, AQAR25. Only AQAR25's register ID is confirmed (F2025L01383). Fetch the others before relying on them.
  • SIL standards, titles are register-verified, but the Rules clauses are TBC and need re-verifying against F2026C00528.
  • 70 versus 66. The register carries 70 standards; 66 are verified, which is what D.json holds and what the navigator searches. Know which number you are using and why.
The standing rule, in the dossier's own words

It is not legal advice, and where anything in it disagrees with the instrument on the Federal Register, the instrument governs. Same posture the engine already takes. And from its developer section: if anything in the dossier fails you, that is a finding, report it and it gets fixed like any other.

The number that makes the register gap concrete

The dossier suite contains 19 registers. The platform schema can hold zero of them. A register is how a provider proves a control actually operates rather than merely existing, and it is the first thing an auditor asks for by name. Right now that entire class of evidence has nowhere to live.

The most interesting problem in the product

ADR 0011 chose flat node linking with inferred document relationships. The dossier declares nine typed relationships and grades every claim Verified, Confirmed, Tested or Draft.

Inference produces a link. It does not produce a defensible reason for the link, and the defensible reason is the product. An auditor does not want to know that a policy is associated with a clause; they want to know it exists to meet it, and they want the citation.

Whether 0011 should now be superseded is your call to make and to write up. If it should stand, say why and we keep it. A well-argued "no" is a good outcome here.

06The work

Three pieces, in this order

FirstADR 0017, before any code

Map the platform's chain against the dossier's, take a position on each of the three problems below, and say which one we build first and why. Written before building so we argue with the reasoning rather than with a migration.

  • Where the two models agree, disagree, and where the dossier is ahead of us.
  • Registers: schema shape, and how they relate to expected documents.
  • Improvement actions: what closes one, and who can close it.
  • Typed relationships versus ADR 0011's inference. Supersede or uphold, with reasons.
Done when the ADR is merged and someone who was not in the room can follow the argument.
SecondRegisters

The 19 register types the dossier specifies, modelled so an auditor can be shown that a control operated on a date, by a person, against a clause. Expect expiry and cadence to matter: the schema already carries expiry_cadence and expiry_on, so look at how those are used before inventing new ones.

Done when a Harmony Care register can be filled in, linked to the clause it serves, and appear in a submission pack.
ThirdImprovement actions

A finding carries forward into a corrective action with an owner, a due date and a closure state, and the loop is traceable in both directions.

Done when a finding can be traced forward to a closed improvement action and backward to the clause that caused it.
07House rules

Constraints you have to work inside

These are already the team's standards, written down in the repo. They are not negotiable and they are not mine, they are April and Ben's, and they are good.

  • No ORM. Kysely over pg, builder API first. A sql`…` fragment is a last resort, must be parameterised, as small as possible, and must carry a one-line comment saying why the builder cannot express it. An unjustified escaped-SQL fragment is a blocking review finding. This applies to any agent you spawn as well.
  • Self-contained migrations (ADR 0008). Each writes its own PK, RLS, policies, grants and tenant contract inline. No shared helpers, no require(). A later edit must never change what an applied migration produced.
  • Row-level security, three roles, rise_app, rise_admin, rise_auth. Anything you add is tenant-scoped by default. Auditor cross-tenant access has its own ADR (0014); read it before touching anything an auditor can see.
  • Tests seed and assert through Kysely over the role pools, not raw pool.query(). test/document-version-state-schema.e2e-spec.ts shows the patterns.
  • Branch <type>/<kebab-desc> off dev, PR into dev, Conventional Commits.
  • The BE owns the API contract. After any DTO change the FE resyncs with pnpm gen:api; never hand-edit app/lib/api/gen/.
08Judgement

How this gets judged

  • The ADR lands first. Code before the argument is the failure mode here.
  • Coverage matches the engine's own standard. 758 lines of spec against 494 lines of service is the bar you are working next to. Nobody expects that ratio everywhere, but obligations logic an auditor relies on gets covered.
  • Provenance survives. Whatever you build, a claim must be able to say where it came from, the way the entity rules still report class 118 even after rewriting the targets.
  • A good "no" counts. If ADR 0011 should stand, the deliverable is the argument, not the migration.

Ask, do not guess

If the dossier and the codebase disagree, that is a real finding and it goes to Jesse rather than getting resolved silently in a migration. The dossier is a specification written by people, not a spec sheet, and parts of it are ahead of what has been agreed commercially.
