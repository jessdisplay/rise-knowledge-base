# The NDIS Compliance System, in Plain English

**Who this is for:** anyone. A new support worker, a board member, a developer building
Rise, a participant's family member. No prior knowledge assumed. **Version 0.1 —
5 July 2026.** The interactive version of everything here is `rise-dossier.html`.

---

## 1. The one-minute version

Parliament made a law. That law lets a Minister and a regulator make detailed rules.
Those rules contain quality standards. Providers write documents that promise to meet
the standards, and keep records that prove they actually do. An independent auditor
checks the records against the standards and reports to the regulator. When any rule
changes, every document that relied on it needs a look. That's the entire system —
everything else is detail.

## 2. Who's who

- **Parliament** made the **NDIS Act 2013**. Everything else gets its authority from
  this Act.
- **The NDIA** (National Disability Insurance Agency) runs the *money* side — plans,
  budgets, payments.
- **The NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission** runs the *safety and quality* side —
  provider registration, standards, complaints, incidents, bans. **The NDIA and the
  Commission are different bodies.** Mixing them up is the most common confusion in
  the sector.
- **Approved quality auditors** are independent audit firms the Commission approves.
  They do the checking; the Commission makes the registration decisions.
- **Providers** deliver supports. **Participants** receive them, and the whole system
  exists to protect their rights and choices.

## 3. The rule stack

Think of it as a ladder. Each rung gets its authority from the rung above:

1. **The NDIS Act 2013** — the law itself.
2. **Rules and Guidelines made under the Act** — the Provider Registration and
   Practice Standards Rules, the Incident Rules, the Complaints Rules, the Restrictive
   Practices Rules, the Worker Screening Rules, the Code of Conduct, the auditor
   scheme. These are "delegated legislation": still law, but written by the executive
   under the Act's authority rather than voted clause-by-clause in Parliament.
3. **The NDIS Practice Standards** — the quality standards themselves. They are not a
   separate booklet with legal force of its own; they live *inside* the Registration
   Rules as schedules. Each standard is one clause of a schedule.
4. **Quality Indicators** — a companion guideline that tells auditors what "meeting
   the standard" looks like in practice.

## 4. Which standards apply to you

Not every provider faces every standard. Two things decide it:

- **What you deliver** (your "registration groups") switches modules on and off. Every
  certified provider does the **Core module** (24 standards across rights, governance,
  service delivery and the support environment). Deliver high-intensity supports and
  **Module 1** switches on. Implement behaviour support plans: **Module 2A**. Write
  them as a specialist: **Module 2**. Early childhood: **Module 3**. Specialised
  support coordination: **Module 4**. Specialist disability accommodation: **Module
  5**. Supported independent living: the new **SIL module**, from 1 July 2026.
- **Risk level** decides the audit type. Higher-risk supports get a **certification
  audit** — document review, site visits, interviews with workers and participants.
  Lower-risk supports get **verification** — a desktop check against a slimmer module
  of four standards (risk, complaints, incidents, HR).

**New in 2026:** SIL providers and platform providers *must* register from 1 July
2026, and providing supports that require registration without being registered is
now a criminal offence. A broader phase-in of mandatory registration is flagged to
run to 2030; exact timing for other categories isn't settled yet.

## 5. How a document proves compliance — the incident story

Follow one thread end to end. The standard says every provider must manage incidents
well (Core module, incident management — one clause in one schedule of the Rules,
backed by the separate Incident Rules that require reporting serious incidents to the
Commission).

1. The provider writes an **Incident Management Policy**: "here is what we promise" —
   safety first, everything recorded, reportable incidents notified, no punishment for
   speaking up. The policy's header states exactly which standard it implements and
   which law it follows.
2. A **procedure** turns the promise into steps: who does what, in what order, by
   when. Statutory deadlines appear in the step where they bite, with their source.
3. A one-page **work instruction** gives the frontline worker the 2 a.m. version:
   make the person safe, tell your manager today, write down what you saw.
4. A **form** captures each incident the same way every time.
5. A **register** logs every completed form — the running proof the process is real.
6. Completed forms and register extracts become **evidence**. The auditor picks a
   standard, asks "show me", and the chain answers: policy → procedure → form →
   register → evidence. If any link is missing, that's the finding.

The single most important audit truth: **a perfect policy with an empty register is a
red flag, not a pass.** Auditors believe registers before they believe prose.

## 6. What an audit actually is

An approved quality auditor reviews your documents, then (for certification) visits
sites and talks to workers and participants — sampling is "opt-out", so participants
are included unless they decline. The auditor reports to the Commission within set
windows (14 days after a verification audit; 28 after certification or mid-term).
A **non-conformity is not a fail** — it's a documented gap with a timeframe to fix
it. Mid-way through a registration cycle there's a smaller **mid-term audit** focused
on governance. The Commission, not the auditor, makes the registration decision.

## 7. What changed in 2026 (and why it matters)

- **SIL Practice Standards** apply from 1 July 2026, written in a new style that pairs
  a provider statement with a **participant statement** ("I am supported to make
  decisions about my home"). Expect future standards to follow this format.
- **The Integrity and Safeguarding Act 2026** (assent 8 April) sharply raises the
  stakes: a new "serious contravention" penalty tier, criminal offences for operating
  unregistered where registration is required or breaching a banning order, banning
  powers extended to auditors and advisors, anti-promotion orders, and stronger
  whistleblower protections — disclosures can now be anonymous, and exposing a
  whistleblower's identity attracts penalties. Providers need a whistleblower policy
  that matches this.
- **Notification duties tightened**: the 2026 Rules changes shorten how long providers
  have to notify the Commission of certain changes and events, and strengthen
  change-of-ownership notice. This applies to *all* registered providers, not just SIL.
- **Another Bill is already before Parliament** (Securing the NDIS for Future
  Generations, introduced May 2026) and the Practice Standards themselves are under
  review. The rules will keep moving; the system for tracking them has to be live.

## 8. Where Rise fits

Rise stores every one of these things — Acts, rules, standards, policies, forms,
registers, evidence, audit findings — as connected pieces, with each connection typed
in plain English ("implements", "puts into practice", "records to", "proves"). Two
payoffs: an auditor's question ("show me how you meet this standard") is answered by
following the links; and when a rule changes, following the links backwards produces
the exact to-do list of documents to review. Every fact also carries an honesty stamp
— verified, corroborated, or still to confirm — so nobody mistakes a draft for law.

## 9. Small glossary

- **Delegated legislation** — law made under an Act's authority rather than passed
  clause-by-clause by Parliament. Still binding.
- **Compilation** — the official merged text of a law with all its amendments applied,
  published on the Federal Register of Legislation.
- **Practice Standard** — one quality requirement a provider is audited against.
- **Quality indicator** — what auditors look for to decide a standard is met.
- **Registration group** — a category of supports a provider registers to deliver;
  determines which modules apply.
- **Certification / verification** — the thorough audit for higher-risk supports vs
  the desktop audit for lower-risk ones.
- **Reportable incident** — a serious incident category that must be notified to the
  Commission within statutory timeframes.
- **Regulated restrictive practice** — a practice limiting a person's rights or
  freedom of movement; only lawful within a behaviour support plan and authorisation,
  with use reported.
- **Non-conformity** — an audit finding that something doesn't yet meet a standard;
  comes with a timeframe to fix.
- **Easy Read** — an accessible document format using simple words and images; the
  Commission publishes Easy Read versions of key material, and participant-facing
  documents in this suite have Easy Read companions planned.

## 10. Honesty box

This guide simplifies on purpose. It is not legal advice. Three simplifications worth
knowing about: (1) module applicability has edge cases the Commission's "initial scope
of audit" resolves per provider; (2) the SIL standards' final consolidated wording
isn't published yet, so SIL detail here follows the Commission's draft; (3) adjacent
laws (privacy, WHS, worker screening, discrimination) are real obligations only
sketched here. Where this guide and an instrument disagree, the instrument wins —
always.
