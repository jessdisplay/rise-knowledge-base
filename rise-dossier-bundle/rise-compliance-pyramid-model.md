# The Rise compliance pyramid — model and complete placement map

**What this is.** The pyramid model for presenting the NDIS compliance stack, plus
the exact position ("address") of every practice standard in it. Verified against
the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Quality Indicators for NDIS Practice
Standards) Guidelines 2018, Compilation No. 1 (15 November 2021), fetched in full
from the Federal Register of Legislation on 6 July 2026.

---

## 1. The seven tiers

| Tier | Layer | Count | Status of the count |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | NDIS Act 2013 | 1 | Verified |
| 2 | Legal instruments (the Rules 2018, the Quality Indicators Guidelines 2018, and the companion rulebooks the indicators name: incident management, complaints, restrictive practices, and SDA conditions rules) | 2 core + at least 4 companions | Verified (companions named inside the indicator text; more exist beyond these) |
| 3 | Modules | 8 (9+ once the 2026 supported independent living module is included) | 8 verified; SIL placement unverified |
| 4 | Practice standards | 66 (70 with SIL, per the Rise build) | 66 verified; the 70 reconciliation is inference |
| 5 | Quality indicators | Several hundred | Not counted — deliberately unstated |
| 6 | Provider documents | 123 | The Rise build's figure — not independently checked |
| 7 | Evidence records | Unbounded, grows daily | By nature uncountable |

## 2. How to use this model — and how not to

**Use it as the lens, not the engine.** The pyramid is the default *view*: authority
flows down, volume grows down, and each layer explains why the one below exists.
But the underlying data must stay a graph, because three verified realities pierce
a strict hierarchy:

1. Indicators name companion rulebooks sideways (e.g. the incident management
   standard requires compliance with the incident rules — a different instrument).
2. The Guidelines cross-reference themselves (the housing tenancy section points
   back to the core service-agreement section).
3. One provider policy legitimately serves many indicators at once — pyramids force
   single parents; reality is many-to-many.

In graph terms: the pyramid is what you see when you render only the "part of" and
"published in" relationships and hide all others. One dataset, one honest hierarchy
view, no duplicate model to maintain. (Design opinion, consistent with the Rise
relationship taxonomy v0.3.)

## 3. The addressing scheme

Every practice standard has a unique address made of three coordinates:

**Module → where the standard lives in the Rules (schedule, clause) → where its
indicators live in the Guidelines (section).**

Example: *Conflict of interest (housing)* = Module 5 → Schedule 7, clause 4 →
section 69. Every pairing below is taken from the eleven statutory mapping tables
in the Guidelines — none are inferred.

## 5. What sits below tier 4

Each standard's indicators (tier 5) are printed in full at its Guidelines section —
the plain-English summaries of all 66 are in the companion document
`ndis-standards-quality-indicators-plain-english-map.md`. Tier 6 (provider
documents) and tier 7 (evidence) are organisation-specific: in the pyramid view,
each provider document hangs beneath every standard it implements — and because one
document typically serves several standards, this is exactly where the pyramid
stops being a tree and the graph takes over.

## 6. Epistemic status

- **Verified:** tiers 1–4, every address in section 4, the reverse-order quirk, and
  the companion rulebooks named in the indicators — all from the instrument text.
- **Inference:** 66 + SIL ≈ 70; the pyramid-as-view recommendation is design
  opinion.
- **Unverified:** the indicator count (uncounted), the 123-document figure (the
  Rise build's number), all SIL placements, and the current 1 July 2026
  compilation, which I have not fetched.
