01The goal

What we are trying to achieve

An NDIS provider should be able to reach audit-ready in one place: see exactly what their registration classes require, prepare and validate the evidence, and know nothing is missing before they lodge. An auditor should be able to complete the audit on-platform: structured access to the same source of truth, comments anchored to the material, conformance recorded against every requirement. That is the whole product in two sentences, and it is why the positioning line is "the audit platform for both sides of the table".

Today neither side has this. Providers stitch the journey across email, spreadsheets, shared drives and a consultant to interpret the requirements. Auditors have no structured workspace at all. The end state we are building toward: the whole loop, discover, prepare, validate, submit, review, outcome, running in one tenant-isolated system, with the compliance content kept current by Rise once, for every tenant at the same time.

The one-line mission: make "are we compliant, and can we prove it?" a question the system answers continuously, instead of a crisis the provider assembles once a registration cycle.

02The engine

Why the audit engine

Everything an auditor does is a version of one question: "show me." Show me the rule you claim to follow (say it), show me how it runs day to day (do it), show me the records proving it really happens (prove it). That question has a shape, and the shape is not a folder tree. It is a graph: a standard is met by a policy, the policy is operationalised by a procedure, the procedure uses a form, the form records to a register, and the register yields the evidence an audit assesses.

Folders and spreadsheets cannot answer "what does my classification require, and am I complete?" because completeness is a property of the graph, not of any document. The moment obligations, documents and evidence are typed nodes and edges, the platform can validate before submission: walk every requirement for the provider's classes and name exactly what is missing or non-conforming before lodgement. That validation is the wedge; it is the thing the folder-and-consultant workflow structurally cannot do.

The engine is not a greenfield idea. The seed graph exists (224 nodes, 528 typed edges, 70 verified standards across 21 instruments, 123 documents), the obligations engine already encodes real statute in code with tests, and the nine-step chain is the spine of both. Steps one to five, standard through register, are populated today. Steps six to nine, evidence, audit, finding, improvement, are what the product brings to life. That is the depth Zain's brief points at, and the reason his brief and this page both start from the same dossier.

03The software

Why it has to be software, and why ours

Because the alternative does not scale and cannot be trusted. Consultants interpret requirements one provider at a time and their work decays the day a rule changes. Generic document tools hold files but know nothing about obligations. A platform holds the rules once, keeps them current once, and every provider inherits the change the moment it lands.

Because the audit itself needs an audit trail. Conformance decisions, document versions, who changed what and when: in a regulated sector these are the product, not features. That is why the non-negotiables were set on day one: tenant isolation at the database row level, immutable version history, Australian data residency, APP and NDB alignment, an ASD Essential 8 baseline, and WCAG 2.2 AA. In a sector handling sensitive participant care data, compliance-grade by design is the moat, hard to retrofit and hard for a generic tool to claim.

Because the graph makes the AI layer honest. Retrieval pipelines and AI-assisted compliance workflows are only safe when they stand on typed, verified structure rather than a pile of PDFs. The knowledge base carries verification tags on every claim precisely so that anything the software asserts can be traced to a source. The AI work in both of your engagements depends on this substrate existing first.

04The bet

The bet, stated so it can fail

From the PRD, unchanged in substance: we believe that giving providers a guided, validated path to audit-readiness, where the platform shows exactly what their classification requires and confirms completeness before submission, will let providers reach audit-ready faster with fewer rejections, and let auditors complete audits on-platform.

Two numbers decide it, measured on real pilot tenants: provider time-to-audit-ready (class selection to submission, against the manual baseline) and first-pass auditor acceptance rate (validated packs that pass review first time). If validated packs do not pass review faster and more often than folder-prepared ones, the wedge is wrong and we rethink. Everything we ship should either move one of those two numbers or protect the compliance-grade foundations that make the product sellable.
